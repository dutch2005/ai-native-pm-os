# Module 10.5 — Final Reflection: Write Your PM AI Principles

**Time:** ~45 minutes  
**Outcome:** A personal, original "PM AI Principles" document — your intellectual signature at the end of this course

---

## Why This Matters

Every framework you've used in this course was someone else's.

RICE was designed at Intercom. Jobs-to-be-Done came from Clayton Christensen. The Pyramid Principle is Barbara Minto's. Even the four-folder structure in Module 0 is an opinionated framework.

Your PM AI Principles are yours. They come from what you've learned, what surprised you, what you agree with, and what you'd do differently.

This document becomes your intellectual fingerprint — something you could hand to a peer, share in a LinkedIn post, or reference in an interview to show how you think.

---

## Your Principles Must Come From You

> **Important:** Do not start with the writing prompt. Start with the reflection prompts.  
> If you hand Claude a blank slate and ask it to "generate your PM AI principles," you'll get a polished document of Claude's principles, not yours. The principles need to be grounded in your actual experience from this course — specific moments, specific surprises, specific decisions you made.  
>
> Write messy, honest answers to the six prompts below. Then bring Claude in to shape, not originate.

---

## The Reflection Prompts

Work through these before writing. Spend at least 5 minutes on each, writing in your own words — rough drafts are fine.

**1. What changed most in how you work?**  
Not "I learned X" — what actually changed in your daily behavior? Be specific.

**2. What do you wish you'd had earlier?**  
The one thing that, if you'd set it up before this course, would have saved you the most time.

**3. What surprised you?**  
One thing you assumed was true that turned out to be wrong or incomplete.

**4. Where do you still feel uncertain?**  
What part of the AI-native PM workflow still feels fragile or unclear for you?

**5. What's your one line on CLAUDE.md?**  
If you had to explain CLAUDE.md's purpose to a PM in one sentence, what would you say?

**6. What's your rule for when to use AI and when not to?**  
Every experienced AI-native PM has a line they won't cross. What's yours?

## The Writing Prompt

Once you've written your answers to the six prompts above (even rough, unpolished notes), bring Claude in to shape them:

```
I've just completed the AI-Native PM OS course.

Here are my raw reflections from the six prompts. These are my actual thoughts — 
unpolished but genuine:

[Paste your answers here — don't clean them up first]

Your job: shape these reflections into 6–8 clear, original principles.
Do NOT generate principles I didn't express — only articulate and sharpen what I wrote.
If a reflection is vague, ask me a follow-up question rather than filling the gap yourself.

Each principle should:
- Have a short title (2–4 words)
- Be written in first-person and present tense
- Be specific enough that a stranger could understand my actual position
- Challenge at least one common assumption about AI in PM work
- Sound like me — reference ABOUT-ME/anti-style.md if it exists

Format: one principle per section, with a 2–3 sentence explanation.

After drafting, show me the list and ask: "Does any of these sound like me explaining
a framework vs. me explaining what I actually believe? Flag the ones that feel off."

Save final version to: CLAUDE-OUTPUTS/capstone/pm-principles.md
```

## What Great PM AI Principles Look Like

Here are examples — not for you to copy, but to show the standard:

> **Work surfaces, not sessions**  
> A session is ephemeral. A surface is cumulative. I invest in the infrastructure that carries context forward — CLAUDE.md files, slash commands, output folders — not in individual conversations that disappear.

> **Critique is the prompt most PMs skip**  
> The difference between a decent PRD and a great one isn't the first draft — it's whether someone challenged it before engineering touched it. I always run the critique before calling anything final.

> **The right model is the cheapest one that doesn't lose quality**  
> I have no loyalty to any model tier. I use Haiku when the task is structured, Sonnet when it's nuanced, and Opus when the stakes justify the cost. Model selection is a financial decision, not a prestige one.

> **I build to end meetings, not to avoid specs**  
> When a conversation keeps going in circles because stakeholders can't visualize something — I build a prototype. Not to skip the spec, but to produce the visual that ends the loop and produces a better spec.

## After Writing Your Principles

**Share them.** Post in the course community. Share on LinkedIn (with appropriate context). Send to a PM peer for discussion.

Your principles are a provocation. They're meant to generate pushback, conversation, and refinement.

**Revisit them in 6 months.** Your principles should evolve as you build more. The best PMs update their principles the way good engineers update their architecture — not constantly, but when something no longer reflects how they actually think.
